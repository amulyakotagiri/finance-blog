      ## Income vs expenses: the rules that make it simple

      When I first started earning ₹4,000, I had no clear plan for managing it.

      Sometimes I'd spend most of it within the first two weeks. Other times I'd hold on to every rupee and feel guilty about spending anything.

      I didn't have a system. I was simply reacting to the money I had.

      This is where budgeting comes in.

      A budget isn't about restricting every little expense or denying yourself everything you enjoy. It's about deciding where your money should go before it disappears into a dozen small purchases.

      And you don't need a complicated spreadsheet to get started. A simple framework can help.

      ## The 50/30/20 rule

      The 50/30/20 rule divides your income into three categories:

      - **50% for needs:** Essential expenses such as food, transport, basic bills, and academic necessities.
      - **30% for wants:** Things you enjoy but could manage without, such as entertainment, shopping, and eating out.
      - **20% for savings:** Money set aside for emergencies, future goals, and, where appropriate, investments.

      For every ₹1,000 you earn, that means ₹500 for needs, ₹300 for wants, and ₹200 for savings.

      Simple, right?

      ## An alternative: the 70/15/15 rule

      If you want to prioritise saving and investing differently, you can experiment with another split:

      - 70% for living expenses
      - 15% for savings
      - 15% for investments

      This is an alternative budgeting template, not a universal financial rule. Whether it works depends on your income, expenses, financial goals, and responsibilities.

      And here's something important: **neither rule is compulsory.**

      As a student, your income might come from a part-time job, an internship, an allowance, or a combination of these. It may change from month to month, while some expenses remain fixed.

      Trying to follow a percentage perfectly when your circumstances change can make budgeting more stressful than helpful.

      Treat these rules as starting points. Adjust them to fit your life.

      ## What I did with my first ₹4,000

      I decided to use the 50/30/20 rule as my starting template, but I adapted it to my own situation.

      Here's how I allocated my money:

      - **50% for expenses — ₹2,000**  
        This went towards transport, academic records, exam fees, and other college essentials.

        - **30% for investments — ₹1,200**  
          I researched stocks using tools such as ChatGPT to understand unfamiliar concepts and *The Economic Times* to follow market developments. I then invested in a few stocks I had shortlisted.

          - **20% for savings — ₹800**  
            I kept this amount aside for future needs instead of spending it immediately.

            You might have noticed something: my allocation wasn't the conventional 50/30/20 split. I used the percentages differently to suit my own priorities.

            And that brings me to an important lesson.

            **A budgeting framework should help you manage your money, not force you into categories that don't fit your circumstances.**

            I won't pretend I got every investment decision right from day one. Researching stocks and learning how to evaluate them is a journey of its own. Investing also carries risk, and putting money into stocks isn't suitable for every financial situation.

            I'll share more about what I learned from that experience in future posts.

            ## Try this yourself

            You don't need a salary to start managing money. Your allowance, internship stipend, freelance income, or part-time earnings are all places to begin.

            Here's a small exercise:

            1. Write down how much money you received last month.
            2. List where that money went.
            3. Group your expenses into needs, wants, savings, and investments.
            4. Compare your actual spending with a budgeting framework such as 50/30/20.
            5. Identify one change you'd like to make next month.

            Don't worry if your spending doesn't match the percentages. That's not a failure. It's information you can use to make better decisions.

            You might discover that a large portion goes towards essentials. Or that small impulse purchases add up more than you expected. Either way, you'll understand your money a little better.
